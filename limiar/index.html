The threshold of when to stop studying

A mathematical model built around a deceptively simple question: when is the best moment to stop a study session? I borrow the Marginal Value Theorem from behavioral ecology — the theory that predicts when a foraging animal should leave a food patch — and treat studying like foraging. You keep gaining correct answers, but at a slowing rate as fatigue sets in. The decisive move is the denominator: the model maximizes correct answers per total cycle time, study plus rest, which is what gives the problem a genuine interior optimum instead of a trivial one.

The rule the model turns on

Treat a study session like foraging in a patch. Let S(t) be the correct answers accumulated after studying for a time t, and let x be the rest planned after it. Instead of maximizing raw output, the model maximizes output per total cycle time, S(t) / (t + x). Taking the derivative and setting it to zero gives the stopping rule:

At the optimum, the instantaneous rate of correct answers equals the average rate over the whole cycle:
S′(t*) = S(t*) / (t* + x).

Geometrically, that is the steepest line drawn from the point (−x, 0) up to a tangent with the accumulation curve — which is exactly the 1976 diagram Eric Charnov drew for the Marginal Value Theorem, with the planned rest x playing the role of travel time between patches.

What I am testing

  • Predicts a falsifiable rule: the longer the planned rest, the longer the optimal session should be — independent of any particular numbers.
  • Its geometry reproduces the classic 1976 Charnov diagram, with rest taking the role of travel time between patches.
  • Open question I am working on: does the optimal threshold keep shrinking cycle after cycle, or settle into a steady state?

The instrument

A model of when to stop studying is worthless without data on how correct answers actually accumulate over time. So I built the tool that collects it: you load a list of exercises, set your study time, rest time and number of cycles, and answer questions one at a time while it records every timestamp and response time. At the end it produces a spreadsheet and fits the accumulation curve — giving back the parameters that feed the model.

A second bench, for higher load

The first continuous session came back with 283 correct out of 285. Accuracy had hit the ceiling, so it stopped measuring anything: with only speed left to vary, the diagnostic that separates fatigue from haste — fatigue slows you down and makes you worse, at the same time — could not be applied at all. The fix is not longer questions, which would empty each five-minute window and drown the estimate in noise; it is questions that chain more rules together while staying about a minute long.

So there is a second bank: 1004 items across 19 labels, every one of them requiring two or more differentiation rules at once — a chain rule inside a root, a quotient over a fractional power, logarithmic differentiation, nested radicals, inverse trigonometric functions. Items sharing a label are deliberately isomorphic: same template, different coefficients. That is what keeps difficulty inside a label homogeneous, and it is measured, not assumed — in the session data, 80% of the variance in solving time sits between labels and only 20% within them.

Third bench: cycles and a vigilance probe

There is a circularity at the centre of any fatigue model built this way. If fatigue is defined as the drop in performance, then saying performance drops because of fatigue is not a hypothesis — it is a restatement. The model has no empirical content. Breaking that circle needs a measure of fatigue that does not come from the study task itself.

So this bench interleaves a psychomotor vigilance probe — a black screen, a counter appearing after a random delay, a key press, repeated for a minute. It has almost no learning curve, and every stimulus is identical, so the difficulty heterogeneity that plagues a bank of exercises simply does not exist in it. With fatigue measured on one channel and performance on the other, the bridge equation becomes falsifiable: it can be wrong.

The probe fires only at the end of a complete cycle through all eight labels — never mid-exercise. That way every interval between probes carries an identical mix of work by construction, so no weighting is needed: the time to close a cycle is directly comparable across the whole session. The order within the cycle is configurable precisely so it can be tested: if shuffling changes nothing in the probe, shuffling is all it ever needed.

Fourth bench: numerical answers

Asking for the derivative as an expression turned out to be the quiet source of most of the measurement trouble. An expression has a form, and form is ambiguous: is 6x(x²+1)² the same answer as the expanded one? The first full session lost its entire opening block to exactly this — fifteen errors, every one of them concentrated in the two structures where the notation rule was least settled, and none of them actually a wrong derivative. It also forced me to grade myself, and a self-applied criterion is free to loosen as tiredness sets in, biasing precisely toward making fatigue disappear from the data.

So this bench asks for a number: the function comes with a point, and the answer is the value of the derivative there. A number has no form. There is nothing to grade, nothing to normalise, no simplification rule to remember, and the app decides correctness on its own. Accuracy also stops pinning to the ceiling by itself — the previous continuous session scored 283 out of 285, which measures nothing at all, whereas evaluating at a point is easy to get wrong even when the derivative is right.

Nothing else about how you work changes: you solve on paper, at your own pace, exactly as in an ordinary study session. Only the answer format is different — a value rather than an expression, which is what an exam question tends to ask for anyway. Every one of the 1016 items across 8 labels was generated so that the derivative lands on an exact rational with a small denominator, verified to forty digits. The arithmetic is kept deliberately light — median answers run from about 1 to 47 — because the evaluation is one step of the item, never the challenge of it. And nothing is revealed while the session runs: no answer, no score, no running accuracy. The count, the curves and the full item list all come at the end, so that knowing how it is going cannot alter what is being measured.

Fifth bench: reading flashcards

The decisive finding of the first four benches was that executing mastered procedures is nearly immune to fatigue — automatic processing is precisely the channel fatigue does not touch. What fatigue degrades is the encoding of new content. So the final bench measures studying in its most literal form: reading passages never seen before and proving, question by question, that they were absorbed.

Each card carries a short self-contained passage (four to five sentences) in didactic sequence. The text appears alone; when the reader finishes, it vanishes and a short question takes its place — answerable in one to three words, from memory, since the text is gone. Nothing is revealed during the session. At the end, every typed answer is compared against the key under a criterion fixed before collection, and the session unfolds into per-cycle charts of rate and accuracy, a continuous-time curve with gaps at the breaks, and the cumulative gain S(t) whose saturating shape the model predicts.

Block boundaries respect the reader: if time expires mid-card, the card is completed first and the overshoot is logged. Study structure is configurable — preset protocols with a five-minute warm-up and pauses of 5, 10, 15 or 20 minutes, or any custom cycle count and duration.
